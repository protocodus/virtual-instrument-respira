# Accordion schools by country (2026-11-24)

How each country's typical accordion is built, tuned and played, what makes
it sound as it does, and what of that Respira's Type control takes in
0.9.0. It widens [the note of 2026-11-19](accordion-construction-by-country-2026-11-19.md),
which covers the eight kinds Type had then. Nearly everything here comes
from tuners, makers, reed makers and players; the two measured sources are
marked. Nothing here was measured on an instrument by us.

## Summary

Three things separate the national sounds, in this order:

1. **The tuning of the 8' reeds against each other.** Every school has its
   own beat, from none to seven a second at A4, and tuners keep a table of
   them (below). It is the first thing a listener places an accordion by.
2. **Which reeds there are and how they are played.** Two voices or five, a
   flat third reed or none, a tone chamber or none, and whether a button
   gives one note or two: a bisonoric box turns its bellows with the tune.
3. **The bass side.** A Stradella bass, a few diatonic buttons, or helikon
   reeds an octave down.

The reeds themselves differ less than their reputation: a measured study of
seven reed blocks in different materials found them within about 1 dB
(Pomberger et al., DAGA 2021, in the earlier note), and reed makers sell
the same classes of reed to every school. The Russian bayan is the
exception, with larger reeds on common plates.

## The tuners' table

Liberty Bellows' list (after Jack Brantley), the sharp 8' reed against the
one at pitch, at A4:

| Beats a second | Cents | Name | Schools |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 0 | unison, dry, secco | classical, Balkan |
| 0.5 | 2 | concert | jazz, tango, Cleveland polka |
| 1 | 4 | swing | gypsy jazz, Brazilian, klezmer |
| 2 | 7 | demi-swing | Irish |
| 2.5 | 10 | American | Cajun, Québécois |
| 3 | 12 | moderate | Slovenian, Tex-Mex, Alpine |
| 4 | 15 | standard | German, Italian |
| 5 | 18 | fast | modern French |
| 6 | 22 | very fast | old French, old Italian |
| 7 | 25 | extremely fast | Scottish |

The same page gives the two ways a tremolo is built. The **German two-reed
tremolo** is one reed at pitch and one sharp: 440 and 444 Hz. The **French
three-reed musette** adds a flat reed: 435, 440 and 445 Hz, beating twice
over. Wikipedia's account of the reed ranks agrees: the flat 8' is "usually
only included on special musette accordions". Hohner says the same of its
own instruments: its three-reed Atlantic IV 120 M is "the Tremolo Atlantic",
built for the French musette's "strong, powerful tremolo".

Players' figures agree with the table where they overlap: "Standard German
tuning is 14 cents. (Standard Italian is 16 cents.)", modern Irish "maybe 6
cents or so", the old Irish and Scottish three-reed boxes 25 to 27
(accordionists.info).

**How the beat changes along the keyboard** (measured and surveyed: Hergert,
*Celeste ranks in pipe organs and accordions*, Forum Acusticum 2023). An
accordion's tremolo runs from 0.5 to 7 Hz at A4. The beat rises with pitch,
linearly or exponentially (doubling about every octave and a half), and
tuners often cap it at 10 to 15 Hz in the treble. A three-reed musette is
set either flat, pitch and sharp by one step each, or pitch, one step and
two steps sharp. Respira's law, the beat as f^0.51, was fitted to a Hohner
and doubles every two octaves, inside that range.

## By school

| School | Typical instrument | Reeds | Tuning at A4 | What is built differently | Heard as |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Italy (Castelfidardo) | piano or button accordion, Stradella bass | LMMH; the 16' and one 8' in a cassotto on the better ones | 15 to 16 cents today, 22 on old ones, one-sided | hand-finished reeds on duralumin plates, waxed to wooden blocks; the cassotto was made to house large 16' reeds | the reference: round, mid-wet |
| Germany (Trossingen, Klingenthal) | Hohner or Weltmeister piano accordion; Club diatonics | LMM or LMMH; three 8' only on the "M" musette models | 14 to 15 cents, two reeds, one-sided | machine reeds; the DIX reed of the Gera tradition has zinc or aluminium plates and a "light tonal setting"; bandoneon plates are a zinc alloy with many tongues | clear, even two-reed tremolo |
| France (Tulle, Paris) | chromatic button accordion (Maugein, Cavagnolo) | MMM for musette, MM otherwise | musette 435/440/445 Hz, 18 to 22 cents; "américain" 1.5 to 2.5 Hz one-sided, Cavagnolo's default; swing | plates nailed or screwed on leather or cork, not waxed; the body unchanged since 1949 at Cavagnolo | the brightest and wettest, or dry and nasal on américain |
| Ireland | two-row B/C or C♯/D button box | MM, or LMM on modern boxes | 6 to 7 cents; old three-reed boxes 25 to 27 | bisonoric: each button gives one note pressed and another drawn; 8 basses, little used | bright, a shimmer, articulated by the bellows |
| Scotland | three-row B/C/C♯ box with a Stradella bass (the Shand Morino) | LMMM | 25 cents and over | a bisonoric treble on a unisonoric bass | the wettest of all |
| Balkans (Serbia) | six-row B-system button accordion (dugmetara), Italian-made | LMMH, double cassotto | dry | hand-made reeds; five bass voices (Scandalli Cromo VI Balkan) | dark, dry, fast and ornamented |
| Russia (Tula) | bayan | up to five voices; MM, MMH and LMMH in use | about 4 cents ("a Bayan has 4 cents tremolo by default") | broader, rectangular reeds, larger for their note, of tougher steel, many to a common plate screwed to the block; a deeper cassotto; much larger bass reeds | "clear treble, hardcore bass", loud, organ-like |
| Alps (Austria, Bavaria, South Tyrol, Slovenia; the Czech heligonka) | Steirische Harmonika, three or four rows | two or three 8' | 12 cents | bisonoric with a Gleichton; **helikon basses**: plates 12 to 15 mm thick, a larger tongue of its own profile; the heligonka's bass reeds sound an octave lower than usual, through a wooden duct | a moderate tremolo over a tuba-like bass |
| Switzerland | Schwyzerörgeli | two or three voices, no registers: the note and its octave, doubled | dry (Bernerton) or lightly tremolo (Innerschweizerton) | bisonoric treble, 18 unisonoric basses | thin, clear, octave-doubled |
| Louisiana | Cajun one-row box | four reeds in octaves (LMMH) on stops | about 10 cents; just intonation in its key | one diatonic row, bisonoric | raw, loud, pure thirds |
| Basque Country | trikitixa, two rows a fifth apart | two voices | not found | bisonoric treble, 12 unisonoric basses | bright, with tambourine |
| Vienna | Schrammelharmonika, B-system buttons | two or three sets in unison | dry | small and light, hand-made reeds, 12 bisonoric basses | quiet, sweet |

**Reed makers' classes** (Harmonikas, Louny, product pages). The classes
are the same for every school and differ in fit: an *a mano* reed has an air
gap of 0.06 mm round the tongue, a machine reed up to 0.13 mm at the tip
and a narrower tongue. The exceptions are sold by name: **bayan** plates in
duralumin 3 to 6 mm thick, brass for the piccolo reeds; **bandoneon**
plates in a zinc alloy with a clearance of 0.03 mm and tongues sized after
German reeds; **helikon bass** plates 15 mm (a mano, gap 0.14 mm) or 12 mm
thick with "a strong, deep tone"; the **DIX** reed for helikons,
Schwyzerörgeli and Viennese models.

**Reed sizes.** The bayan is the one school with a source for larger
reeds: "generally larger (for the same note)... the difference is smaller
on the very highest notes" (Paul De Bra, accordionists.info), and one
poster's "a C2 bayan reed is 10 cm and the Italian is 7 cm (both approx)".
No source gives tongue sizes for German, French, Irish or Balkan reeds, so
Respira's builds keep the default's.

**Press and draw.** A diatonic row gives its key's major triad pressed and
the rest of its scale drawn. On a B/C box that is C, E and G pressed and D,
F, A and B drawn on the C row, and the same a semitone lower on the B row,
so E and B come both ways and every other note one way only. "The rhythmic
effects inherent in the push-pull action are very well suited to the lively
rhythms of dance music"; on semitone-apart boxes, depending on the key,
"players may be obliged to adopt a smoother style" (Wikipedia, Diatonic
button accordion).

## What Respira takes in 0.9.0

| Type | Change | From |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Balkan | tuned dry: both 8' in unison, where it had the second 15 cents sharp | the tuners' table. The 15 was read off one sampled set in the owner's comparison clip (9 to 18 cents). |
| German | four voices, LMMH, with the two-reed tremolo at 15 cents; the flat third reed is gone | the tuners' German tremolo, the reed ranks account, Hohner's own naming of its three-reed model |
| Irish | Auto presses and draws the bellows as a B/C box gives each note | the diatonic row's layout |
| all three | a tremolo reed is tuned against its key's 8' reed, with an error that is a share of its detune | a tuner sets a beat, not two pitches. The share, 4.6 cents in 18, is a played Hohner's (`Tools/measure_musette.py`); that it scales with the detune is assumed. |

With each reed's own 3 cent error, as before 0.9.0, the pairs from C4 to
C6 would beat at −0.2 to 2.8 times their law on Irish and 0.5 to 1.8 times
on German, and a dry pair at up to 5 Hz; now they lie between 0.57 and 1.43
times their law, and a dry pair does not beat.

The four types now stand on four rungs of the tuners' table: Default 22
cents with a three-reed musette, German 15 on two reeds, Irish 7, Balkan 0.

## What it leaves

- **German stays the nearest to Default.** Default's reeds were fitted to a
  Hohner and an Italian instrument, and Default's Violin Folk preset is a
  two-reed tremolo at 14 cents already, which is what German Club plays.
  German differs where its reed sets do: no three-reed musette, and a
  Master of four voices with dry octaves. Nothing else that is sourced
  separates a Hohner from the default instrument in this model.
- **The bass side is the same for every type.** The helikon bass, a bayan's
  bass reeds and an Irish box's few light basses are the largest
  differences not modelled.
- **Not built as types:** Russian bayan (larger reeds, about 4 cents, the
  one school whose reed size has a source), Alpine with helikon basses,
  French, Italian, Scottish, Swiss and Cajun. French and Italian were types
  until 0.8.0.
- **A real B/C box turns its bellows faster** than Auto's hand does here:
  every type's bellows hold the same air, so a small box is no stiffer. At
  reel speed a note that needs a turn speaks 20 to 30 ms late and 1.5 dB
  softer on average.
- **Just intonation** (Cajun) and each school's own beat law along the
  keyboard are not modelled: every type follows the default's f^0.51.
